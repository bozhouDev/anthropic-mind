New research result: we use Claude to make fully autonomous progress on scalable oversight research, as measured by performance gap recovered (PGR).

Claude iterates on a number of different techniques and ends up significantly outperforming human researchers for $18k in credits.

Automating alignment research (AAR) can be bottom-up (researchers automate more and more of their existing work), or top-down (we carve up specific subproblems for AI to solve). Most of our work is the former (e.g. using Claude for coding), but this work is about the latter.

We frame progress on scalable oversight similar to weak-to-strong generalization: what fraction of the performance of a strong model trained on “golden” data can be recovered when training the strong model only using a weak supervision signal?

In this case, Claude develops scalable oversight methods on chat reward modeling datasets and evaluates them on math and code datasets. The best methods do really well on math, but are more mixed on code. This suggests Claude’s methods were overfit to the data and models we used.

Moreover, even in this constrained setup, our AARs tried to hack the metric: e.g. one skipped the weak teacher entirely after noticing the most common answer was usually right. We caught these, but it's a warning for future AARs whose hacks may be harder to catch.

These AARs can also be applied to other alignment research projects that are “crisp”, i.e. where we can procedurally verify the quality of the work: automated red teaming, auditing games, control methods, etc.

However, most alignment research is not very crisp and needs research taste to evaluate. That is why we pointed AAR at this scalable oversight problem: progress here lets AAR take on fuzzier alignment problems where humans can only give weak supervision.
