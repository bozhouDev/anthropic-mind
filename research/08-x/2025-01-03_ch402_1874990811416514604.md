This had lots of effects – one of them (which I benefited from!) was that it was very easy to transition into deep learning from another field.

## 2025-01-03 01:26:59 · ch402 · 1874990810099572946

As @kchonyc observes, the deep learning job market was in a very strange place circa 2014. Deep learning went from being very obscure in 2012 (probably &lt;&lt;100 people working on it seriously), to being very in demand.

## 2025-01-03 01:26:59 · ch402 · 1874990808539275687

@kchonyc wrote a thoughtful note on the AI job market. I think he gets some important things right. Other things seem different from my perspective.

## 2025-01-03 01:27:00 · ch402 · 1874990815652823231

(Lots of caveats here. 

Of course, industry – at this point, large tech companies – wanted to do great research because it expected to benefit, although there were altruistic motivations as well. Expected benefits included believing the benefits of progress would just generally accrue to them, having a lead and in-house expertise on new discoveries if they're made there first, using researchers as recruiting beacons for more applied teams, … 

I also don't mean to say that the preferences of researchers, who often cared deeply about authorship, weren't a major factor.)

## 2025-01-03 01:27:00 · ch402 · 1874990814159614350

@kchonyc hypothesizes this was because industry was stocking up on talent and giving concessions to get people.

I’m inclined to think industry labs just wanted to do great research, and adopted the model that the massive influx of academic hires brought with them.

## 2025-01-03 01:27:00 · ch402 · 1874990812771307927

Initially, industry labs (especially Google Brain) tended to operate in a grass roots, individual research freedom style typical of academia. Researchers had a lot of freedom on direction, possibly collaborating. Authorship and citation considerations were very influential.

## 2025-01-03 01:27:01 · ch402 · 1874990819662495954

There are just some domains and problems that really benefit from focused bets. This isn’t unique to AI or industry – see CERN, LIGO, JWST, etc in physics.

## 2025-01-03 01:27:01 · ch402 · 1874990818282582038

I think the “focused bet / team science” model has significantly won out over the “grassroots / individual” model, and this is one of the changes people are perceiving.

## 2025-01-03 01:27:01 · ch402 · 1874990816994926661

But another model of industry AI lab started to gain prominence, especially at DeepMind and OpenAI. I’ll call it the “focused bet / team science” model. Here, the lab decides it wants to make a bet on some direction and staffs a team with that mandate.

## 2025-01-03 01:27:02 · ch402 · 1874990823580008938

(Aside: My take on the “mood” by which scaling is beautiful and fascinating science - https://x.com/ch402/status/1533164918886703104 )

## 2025-01-03 01:27:02 · ch402 · 1874990822258823237

This transition was tightly coupled to research agenda. So at the same time, the research agenda of academia and industry began to diverge more and more.

In particular, academia was dismissive of scaling while industry bet hard on it.

## 2025-01-03 01:27:02 · ch402 · 1874990820979605679

But in AI, it was structurally much easier for industry to transition to the focused bet model. So industry did, while academia largely didn’t.

## 2025-01-03 01:27:03 · ch402 · 1874990829049413676

Re (1) - In team science:

You lose the ability to unilaterally set research direction. You gain the ability to influence the direction of a larger group.

You lose the guarantee of first authorship on a paper. You gain being part of a much larger project.

## 2025-01-03 01:27:03 · ch402 · 1874990827761807541

So if you’re an academic considering industry research roles, I’d offer the following questions and frame:

(1) Would you enjoy working in the team science / focused bet model?
(2) What bets would you be excited to be a part of?

I’ll talk through these below.

## 2025-01-03 01:27:03 · ch402 · 1874990826545356955

This research agenda decoupling is another thing people feel – if you are working on a topic in academia which industry is less focused on, you’ll feel this if you then try to transition to industry.

## 2025-01-03 01:27:03 · ch402 · 1874990825257775602

I think causality goes many directions here. Industry changed models to better pursue the research agenda people were excited about. Academia was more skeptical of scaling because it was structurally hard for them to pursue (incentive structure, mechanisms for focusing funding, hiring engineers, etc)...

## 2025-01-03 01:27:04 · ch402 · 1874990833084285409

But all of this depends on finding somewhere in industry that’s making a bet you believe in and want to be a part of.

## 2025-01-03 01:27:04 · ch402 · 1874990831775760661

If you find the right home in industry, you can have incentives that are much more aligned with “just do good science”.

There can also be a strong sense of shared mission, if everyone believes in the bet (and especially if they believe there's a reason it matters).

## 2025-01-03 01:27:04 · ch402 · 1874990830538379337

You also get freed from a lot of the incentives of academia. You don’t need to worry about the number of publications, or authorship order, or grants, or what reviewers will think.
